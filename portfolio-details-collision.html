Predictive Collision Avoidance for Urban E-Scooters

Dual-Metric TTC/DRAC Safety Algorithm - ARM Cortex-M4 Ready

The Problem

E-scooter collision fatalities in the UK rose sharply between 2020 and 2022. Existing safety research focused heavily on automotive-scale vehicles - the specific dynamics, speeds, and urban encounter geometries of micro-mobility were largely unaddressed. Most existing TTC-only algorithms also share a critical blind spot: they can fail to trigger an emergency warning in scenarios where the physical braking demand (DRAC) already makes collision unavoidable, even when the time-to-impact still appears safe.

The challenge was to design a collision avoidance algorithm specifically calibrated to UK e-scooter conditions - using real accident data rather than borrowed automotive thresholds - and to prove it was fast enough to run on a real embedded microcontroller.

What I Built

I designed and implemented a dual-metric collision avoidance algorithm in portable C99, combining Time-to-Collision (TTC) and Deceleration Rate to Avoid Collision (DRAC). The dual-metric design is intentional: TTC measures temporal imminence (how long until impact?), while DRAC measures physical inevitability (how hard must we brake right now?). Scenarios exist where each metric alone fails - the combination provides coverage that a TTC-only system cannot match.

Every 10 milliseconds, the algorithm takes the scooter's position, heading, and speed, plus the detected obstacle's position and velocity, and outputs five values: TTC in seconds, DRAC in m/s², a 3-state safety status (SAFE / CAUTION / EMERGENCY), a braking command, and a steering correction angle.

Threshold derivation was data-driven, not borrowed. I analysed UK STATS19 e-scooter accident data from 2020-2022 to derive the decision thresholds directly from real UK conditions. At 25 km/h with maximum braking (3.5 m/s²), minimum stopping distance is 6.9 m, giving a minimum TTC at the unavoidability boundary of ~1.0 s. The 1.5 s emergency threshold provides a 0.5 s margin above this. The 3.0 s caution threshold equals braking time plus reaction time at maximum legal UK speed.

The MATLAB/Simulink SIL framework validated the algorithm across 500 structured scenarios spanning 5 encounter types: head-on (25%), crossing path (30%), emergency cut-in (20%), overtaking (15%), and near-miss glancing passes (10%). The near-miss scenarios specifically test false-positive rejection - a system that cries emergency on every near-miss is useless in practice. Monte Carlo seed was fixed at rng(42) for full reproducibility.

The C algorithm was written specifically for embedded deployment - no dynamic memory allocation, no recursion, no platform dependencies beyond <math.h>, C99 standard throughout. It cross-compiles directly to ARM Cortex-M4 with GCC ARM toolchain.

Key Finding from Sensitivity Analysis

Ego speed is the dominant parameter governing safety margin - a 10% speed reduction produces a larger safety improvement than a 30% improvement in sensor noise. This finding has a direct policy implication: speed regulation contributes more to collision avoidance effectiveness than sensor hardware upgrades. This makes the algorithm directly relevant to UK micro-mobility regulation discussions.
